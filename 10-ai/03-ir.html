Capturing and Applying an Impulse Response (IR)

A brief, simplified outline of the DSP behind measuring an impulse response with a microphone and a sine chirp, and of applying the captured IR to a signal.

Capturing the IR (exponential sine sweep / Farina method)

  1. Generate the excitation. Use an exponential (log) sine sweep x(t)x(t): instantaneous frequency rises geometrically from f1f_1 to f2f_2 over duration TT. Pad with trailing silence to leave room for the system’s decay tail. The exponential (rather than linear) sweep is the key choice — its spectrum is pink (≈−3\approx -3 dB/octave), and it has a clean analytic inverse.

  2. Play and record. Send x(t)x(t) out through the system under test (DAC →\to amp →\to speaker →\to room/device →\to mic →\to ADC) and record y(t)y(t). What you’ve recorded is y=x*hy = x \ast h, where hh is the IR of the entire chain. If you only want the room (or only the device), measure the speaker+mic path separately and deconvolve it out, or use a reference loopback.

  3. Build the inverse filter. Time-reverse the sweep and apply an amplitude envelope that compensates its spectral tilt (a +6+6 dB/octave correction, falling from start to end) so that x*f=δx \ast f = \delta. This f(t)f(t) is a regularized deconvolution kernel — it behaves far better at the band edges than naive spectral division Y/XY/X.

  4. Deconvolve. Convolve the recording with the inverse filter:

    y*f=(h*x)*f=h*(x*f)=h*δ=hy \ast f = (h \ast x) \ast f = h \ast (x \ast f) = h \ast \delta = h

    Out pops the impulse response. The payoff of the exponential sweep: harmonic distortion products land at negative times (they’re time-advanced relative to the linear IR), so they appear as separate impulses ahead of the main response and can simply be windowed off — yielding a clean, distortion-free linear IR.

Practical knobs: longer T→T \to better SNR; pick f1f_1/f2f_2 a little beyond your band of interest; window the tail to set the decay length you actually keep.

Applying the IR to a signal

This is just convolution: sout=sin*hs_\text{out} = s_\text{in} \ast h.

  1. Choose the method by IR length and latency budget.
    • Short IR →\to direct time-domain convolution is fine.
    • Long IR (reverb tails, cabinet/room sims) →\to FFT-based fast convolution: block the input, multiply spectra by H=ℱ{h}H = \mathcal{F}\{h\}, inverse-FFT, and stitch with overlap-add or overlap-save.
    • Real-time / low-latency →\to partitioned convolution: split hh into blocks so the first (small) partition gives low input-to-output latency, while larger later partitions keep the cost down. Non-uniform partitioning is the usual production choice.
  2. Normalize. Scale hh (unity gain, or to taste) so the convolution doesn’t blow up the level — long IRs accumulate a lot of energy.

The whole loop: sweep →\to record →\to deconvolve with the inverse filter →\to window →\to convolve your dry signal with the result.
