*Plain-language version, for an audience new to AI and light on signal processing. Just enough DSP for flavor, no heavy math.*

---

## The single idea to carry through

Four moves keep coming back:

1. **Get the sound ready** — clean it up into a tidy stream of numbers.
2. **Tokenize** — boil that stream down to a short list of "sound IDs."
3. **Learn** — train a model (built on *autoencoders*) to find the patterns.
4. **Steer** — point it with a text prompt.

And the punchline: **generation and analysis are the same machinery pointed in opposite directions.** Once that clicks, the rest is detail.

## Block 1 — From sound to tokens (17 min)

### First, a little signal processing: how sound becomes numbers (~5 min)

- **A recording is just amplitude, sampled fast.** A microphone tracks the speaker-cone position; we measure it at a fixed rate — a CD does 44,100 times a second. Each measurement is one *sample*: a single number. That's it. A few seconds of audio is already hundreds of thousands of numbers.
- **Before any AI touches it, the audio gets tidied up** so every clip looks the same to the model:
  - **Resample** everything to one standard rate (MusicGen uses 32,000/sec).
  - **Mono or stereo** — pick one and stick with it.
  - **Normalize loudness**, so a whisper-quiet track and a loud one are on equal footing.
  - **Cut into fixed-length chunks** (MusicGen trains on 30-second pieces).
- **Optional flavor:** some systems first turn the waveform into a *spectrogram* — a heatmap showing which pitches are sounding, and how loud, over time. It's the closest thing to "sheet music the computer can see," and it's especially common on the *analysis* side. (MusicGen's tokenizer skips this and works on the raw samples.)

That cleaned-up stream of numbers is what feeds the tokenizer.

### Then, the tokenizer (~8 min)

- **Start somewhere familiar: ChatGPT.** A *text* tokenizer chops a sentence into small chunks ("tokens") and gives each a number. The model never sees letters — only lists of numbers.
- **Music isn't words, and hundreds of thousands of samples a second is far too many to model directly.** So an **audio tokenizer** learns a modest "dictionary" of little sound-pieces and rewrites a song as a short sequence of dictionary IDs — *dozens per second* instead of tens of thousands. And it can turn that sequence back into audio.
- **Analogy:** a box of LEGO "sound bricks." Any song becomes a sequence of brick choices. (Under the hood it's a few stacked dictionaries layered for detail, but the picture is enough.)
- **Why it matters:** this one move — sound → short list of IDs → sound — turns an impossible problem into a manageable one.

> **Play this here:** the EnCodec page lets you hear the original vs. the version rebuilt from nothing but its tokens — the tokenizer made audible. <https://audiocraft.metademolab.com/encodec.html>

Links: EnCodec — <https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.13438> · SoundStream — <https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.03312>

### What the token stream actually looks like (~4 min)

This is worth a slide, because "stream" is misleading — it sounds like one sound after another, but music has many things going on at once. The resolution: the tokens aren't a single line, they're a small **grid**.

- **Time runs across; at each instant there's a short stack of codes** (MusicGen uses four), from a coarse code that captures the gist down to finer codes that add detail.
- **Each column is one ~20 ms snapshot of the *whole* mix** — guitar, bass, vocal, cymbal, all summed together. So "everything at once" is captured *inside* a single slice, not stretched out along the line. The only thing that actually moves left-to-right is time.
- **A token here is not a note** — that's the usual sticking point. There's an older style (the "symbolic," MIDI-like approach) where a token really *is* a note, so a chord becomes several note-tokens in a row. Audio tokenizers do the opposite: one stack of codes per time-slice of the full, mixed sound.
- *Tie-back:* what you heard "rebuilt from tokens" on the EnCodec demo is exactly this grid decoded back into audio — the coarsest code alone sounds rough; all four levels together sound clean.

## Block 2 — Autoencoders & how learning happens (10 min)

The tokenizer from Block 1 is built as an **autoencoder**. The idea is simpler than the name.

- **An autoencoder is a network trained to copy its input back to itself — through a deliberately narrow middle.** An *encoder* squeezes the input down to a tiny summary (the "code"); a *decoder* expands it back out. Training just nudges the network's knobs until the output looks like the input again.
- **Why the narrow middle is the whole trick:** to rebuild something from very little, the network must keep only what matters and toss the rest. It learns the *essence* on its own. Analogy: describe a photo in ten words so a friend can re-sketch it — you're forced to keep what's important.
- **The audio tokenizer is just an autoencoder whose middle is forced to be a list of dictionary IDs** (discrete numbers) instead of a smooth summary. Encoder = "sound → tokens," decoder = "tokens → sound."
- **The big payoff for learning — no labels needed.** The input is its own correct answer, so you can train on enormous piles of plain, unlabeled music. (That's "self-supervised" learning, and it's why these systems can swallow so much data.) The compact code it learns also doubles as a reusable *understanding* of audio that the analysis tasks in the next block borrow.

Links: gentle autoencoder walk-through — <https://blog.keras.io/building-autoencoders-in-keras.html> · the audio version in practice (EnCodec) — <https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.13438>

## Block 3 — The model: transformers, synthesis, analysis, and steering (16 min)

Now music is a short sequence of tokens — basically a little language. So we reuse the model behind modern chatbots: the **transformer**.

### What a transformer does, briefly (~4 min)
- A transformer reads a sequence and is very good at one job: **"given everything so far, what comes next?"**
- Its key trick is **attention**: when it's working on one spot in the sequence, it can look back at *all* the earlier spots and decide which ones matter right now. That's how it tracks long-range structure — like making a chorus echo a verse from thirty seconds earlier. (For the curious: the Illustrated Transformer — <https://jalammar.github.io/illustrated-transformer/>)

### Synthesis — making music (~5 min)
- Show the transformer mountains of music-as-tokens until it has absorbed which tokens tend to follow which.
- To generate, hand it a starting context and let it **autocomplete one token at a time**. Run the finished token sequence back through the decoder → a brand-new song. It's autocomplete, for sound. (MusicGen does this roughly 50 tokens per second of audio.)

### Steering — how the text prompt actually works (~4 min) *(the part worth dwelling on)*
- Your words don't go straight into the music model. They first pass through a separate **text-reading network** (a text encoder, e.g. Google's T5) that turns the prompt into a list of vectors — numbers that capture *meaning*, so "sax" and "saxophone" land near each other.
- As the music transformer picks each sound-token, it **looks at those prompt vectors and leans its choice toward sounds that match** ("attention," again — this time pointed at the prompt). At every single step, it's glancing back at "mellow jazz with sax" and biasing what it emits.
- It learned to do this by training on **(text description, music) pairs**, so it picked up the associations between words and sounds.
- There's even a **strength knob** ("guidance"): turn it up and the model obeys the prompt more literally; turn it down and it wanders more freely. You'll see this exact slider in the demos.

### Analysis — understanding music (~3 min)
- The same kind of model — or just the encoder's compact code from Block 2 — can instead produce an *answer about* a piece: its genre, its tempo, or even pulling the vocals out of a mix. Vaguely: instead of predicting the next sound, it predicts a *fact* about the sound.
- *Audible example:* **source separation** — splitting a finished song back into vocals / drums / bass / other. **Demucs** does this remarkably well and is great to play.

### One honest caveat & where Suno/Udio sit
- There's a second popular approach, **diffusion**, that works differently — it starts from noise and repeatedly cleans it into music (Stable Audio uses it). We won't get into it; the autocomplete picture is enough.
- Suno and Udio are **closed** — no published recipe — but they're assembled from exactly these parts: a tokenizer, an autocomplete-style transformer over the tokens, a prompt encoder to steer it, and a separate text model for lyrics. The giant "billions of parameters / millions of hours" figures online are mostly unverifiable marketing.

Links: MusicGen — <https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05284> · code — <https://github.com/facebookresearch/audiocraft> · Demucs — <https://github.com/facebookresearch/demucs> · Suno CEO interview — <https://www.amplifypartners.com/barrchives/how-suno-builds-ai-models-for-musicians>

## Block 4 — Ethics, copyright, and labor (15 min)

Timely and genuinely unsettled — exactly what a closing lecture should leave open. Frame it as live; tell students to track it themselves.

- **Training-data copyright is the central fight.** In June 2024 the recording industry (Universal, Sony, Warner, via the RIAA) sued both Suno and Udio, alleging the models were trained on copyrighted recordings without permission. The companies argue training is transformative **fair use**.
- **It's moving fast.** As of mid-2026: Universal **settled** with Udio (Oct 2025) and announced a jointly built, *licensed* AI-music platform for 2026; Warner reached settlement(s) in late 2025; **Sony has not settled** and is still in court. A **fair-use ruling is expected in summer 2026** that could set precedent for the whole field — a win for the AI side guts the labels' leverage; a loss makes licensing mandatory. Separately, **independent artists** filed their own class actions (late 2025), arguing the big-label deals do nothing for smaller musicians.
- **Beyond training data:** voice cloning and likeness rights; artist consent and pay; flooding streaming services with machine-made tracks; and watermarking / provenance of AI audio.
- **Discussion seed:** is "the model learned from it" meaningfully different from "the model copied it"? Tie it back to Blocks 1–2 — what's actually *stored* in the network is a dictionary of generic sound-pieces and statistical patterns, not the songs themselves. Is that enough to be fair use?

## Block 5 — Wrap, discussion, and demos (6 min)

- **One-line recap:** clean the sound into numbers, boil it down to tokens, train an autocomplete over the tokens, steer it with a prompt — and analysis is the same idea aimed at producing an answer instead of a song.
